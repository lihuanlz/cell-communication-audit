## Abstract

Living cells sense and encode information with biochemical circuits, yet what can in principle be inferred about these circuits from measurable outputs remains poorly characterized. Here we transfer an identifiability audit—developed for bioanalytical assays, where it established that digital counting is calibration-free—to cellular information circuits. We audit the smallest static sensor, the phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle, and the canonical dynamic encoder, the p53 pulse generator. For each, we derive the degeneracy group of parameters that leave all measurable outputs invariant, quantify the Fisher-information barrier separating identifiable circuit structure from unidentifiable molecular scales, and establish one-sided reporting protocols for parameters at the edge of identifiability. For the pulse generator, we prove that the pulse observables themselves select circuit topology: uniform-amplitude, dose-counting pulses require an excitable architecture containing positive feedback, excluding pure negative-feedback models as sufficient explanations of the p53 data. Across both the static and dynamic cases, the calibration-free regime is the same mathematical limit: when the analog scale factor cancels, information survives only in counting statistics. This shared digital limit—realized chemically in assays and topologically in cells—explains why evolution, like assay engineers, encodes dose in discrete events.

## 1. Introduction

Cells are information processors. They sense extracellular and intracellular signals through biochemical circuits and encode the results in the dynamics of their molecular states [Tkačik & ten Wolde, 2025]. Two circuit modes implement the two fundamental operations. Static sensors—exemplified by the phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle (PdPC), the covalent-modification motif at the heart of kinase signaling [Goldbeter & Koshland, 1981]—convert an input level into an output level through a steady-state input–output relation. Dynamic encoders—exemplified by the p53 tumor suppressor, which responds to DNA damage with discrete, stereotyped pulses whose number reports dose [Lahav, 2004; Batchelor, 2008]—convert an input level into a temporal pattern. A mature information-theoretic literature quantifies how much information such circuits transmit [Tostevin & ten Wolde, 2009; Govern & ten Wolde, 2014; Selimkhanov, 2014; Bowsher & Swain, 2014], and a mature phenomenological literature catalogues their dynamics [Geva-Zatorsky, 2006; Purvis, 2012].

A prior question has received far less systematic attention: **what can be inferred about the circuit itself from what experiments can measure?** The kinetic parameters of cellular circuits are inferred from output statistics—dose–response curves, pulse periods, amplitudes, counts—yet it is now well documented that very different parameter sets can produce nearly identical circuit behavior [Prinz, 2004; Goaillard & Marder, 2021]. This degeneracy is usually reported as an empirical observation about specific models. What is missing is an audit discipline: a systematic procedure that takes a circuit model and its measurable observables, and returns a rigorous stratification of what is identifiable (which parameter combinations), what is boundable (which parameters admit one-sided limits), and what is structurally inaccessible (which directions of parameter space leave every observable invariant)—together with reporting protocols for each case.

We recently developed such an audit for a different class of information systems: bioanalytical assays [TCS paper, this work]. There, the audit of the binding equilibrium produced a sharp result with practical consequences: assay platforms fall into two regimes separated by a saturation parameter κ, and only in the digital limit κ → 0—where each molecule contributes a discrete counting event—does the measurement become calibration-free, because the analog scale factors cancel exactly from the observable statistics. Digital PCR, which counts partitions, occupies this limit; analog platforms such as ELISA and single-molecule-array counting of calibration curves do not.

Here we transfer the audit from assays to cellular circuits, asking whether the same discipline yields results—and whether the same digital limit appears. We examine the two canonical circuits above, one static and one dynamic. Three contributions emerge. **First**, for both circuits the audit is fully constructive: we derive the degeneracy groups exactly, quantify the Fisher-information barrier (identifiable *structure* versus unidentifiable *scale*, separated by condition numbers of ~30 versus ~10^300 in the static case), and give one-sided reporting protocols for parameters in the deep-saturation regime where only bounds are meaningful. **Second**, for the p53 pulse generator we prove a topology-selection theorem: the joint dose-dependence of pulse amplitude and pulse count is a signature that discriminates circuit universality classes—uniform-amplitude dose-counting requires an excitable architecture with positive feedback, excluding pure negative-feedback models as sufficient explanations, consistent with the known presence of interlinked positive loops in the p53–ATM–Mdm2–Wip1 network. **Third**, across the static and dynamic cases the calibration-free regime is the same mathematical limit: whenever the analog scale factor cancels—by chemical saturation in the assay (κ → 0) or by circuit topology in the encoder (the amplitude–dose decoupling α → 0)—the likelihood reduces to counting statistics and only dimensionless ratios survive. Cells, like assay engineers, count when they cannot calibrate.

The results bear on two active discussions. To the information-theoretic literature on biochemical circuits [Tkačik & ten Wolde, 2025], which quantifies how much information flows, the audit adds the complementary question of which circuit parameters the flow can reveal—and shows that the celebrated zero-order ultrasensitive switch is information-poor as a steady-state channel, with the information-optimal cycle sitting at intermediate saturation matched to its input distribution. To the literature on parameter degeneracy in neuronal and cellular models [Prinz, 2004; Goaillard & Marder, 2021], which reads degeneracy as a substrate for robustness, the audit adds the formal structure—the degeneracy group—and the observation that the same group is a measurement blind spot from outside and a design space from inside.

## 2. The identifiability audit

**Four parameter sets, one curve.** Consider the phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle of §3, with six kinetic parameters (two catalytic rates, the Michaelis constant, two enzyme totals, substrate total). Set A assigns them (1, 1, 0.1, 1, 1, 1) in consistent units. Set B doubles both catalytic rates; set C multiplies K_m and substrate total by five; set D triples k₁ and the phosphatase total while doubling K_m and substrate total. Every individual parameter changes, by up to five-fold. Yet the four steady-state dose–response curves are identical—not similar, but equal to the last digit of double-precision arithmetic (max |Δu| = 0 over the full titration range; Fig. 1b). No experiment on this curve, at any precision, can tell the four sets apart. What, then, can fitting possibly return? Not the six parameters—at most what the four sets share. What they share is exactly two combinations: the saturation ratio κ = K_m/S_T and the catalytic-balance factor c = k₁E_1T/k₂E_2T. Everything else about the parameter vector is invisible.

**Names for what was just seen.** This is the general situation, and it has a calculus. A circuit is a dynamical model M(θ), θ ∈ R^p; experiments observe a vector of *observable functionals* O(θ) (dose–response coordinates, pulse periods, amplitudes, counts, widths, interval distributions). The transformations connecting indistinguishable parameter sets form the **degeneracy group** G = {g : O(g·θ) = O(θ) ∀θ}; its dimension is the number of parameter directions invisible to these observables (four in the example above: 6 parameters − 2 shared combinations). The shared combinations themselves are the **identifiable combinations**—the only objects an experiment on these observables can return. Identifiability is therefore not a property of parameters but of parameter *combinations*, and the first task of inference is to find them.

**The audit.** The identifiability audit is the systematic execution of this finding, in five steps:

1. **Master equation.** Write the input–output relation in dimensionless form, exposing the minimal parameter groups that govern the observables—nondimensionalization is not cosmetic but diagnostic: it is how the identifiable combinations are found.
2. **Degeneracy group.** Determine G and its dimension—the exact count of invisible directions.
3. **Fisher barrier.** Compute the Fisher information over the observable likelihood at experimentally realistic noise; stratify parameter directions into *identifiable* (well-conditioned), *sloppy* (ill-conditioned but bounded), and *degenerate* (zero eigenvalue—exactly unidentifiable, the directions of G). Sloppiness is cured by data; degeneracy is not.
4. **One-sided protocols.** Where the likelihood plateaus, replace the point estimate with a scan-based one-sided bound defined by a goodness-of-fit criterion that is invariant across optimizer implementations (report κ ≥ κ_min or κ ≤ κ_max, never a spurious point value).
5. **Orthogonal resolution.** Identify which additional measurement axis, if any, breaks the degeneracy—degenerate need not mean unknowable, only invisible to the observables at hand.

**The fingerprint.** The audit's output is a three-tier stratification—identifiable combinations, one-sided bounds, degenerate directions—each with an explicit reporting protocol. Both circuits below produce exactly this structure, which we take as the framework's fingerprint. We note one consequence here and develop it in the Discussion: because observables depend only on identifiable (dimensionless) combinations, the functionally complete description of a circuit lives in dimensionless coordinates; the absolute molecular scales are a redundant gauge—and, correspondingly, the dimensionless groups are where perturbation experiments, cross-system comparisons, and evolutionary constraints alike must be stated.

## 3. The static sensor: auditing the phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle

*(Results section 2 — PdPC; proofs deferred to SI)*

**The cycle and its master equation.** The Goldbeter–Koshland cycle interconverts substrate S between unmodified (S) and modified (S*) forms by two enzymes E₁ (kinase) and E₂ (phosphatase) [Goldbeter & Koshland, 1981]. With six physical parameters (the two catalytic rates, the Michaelis-m constant ratio, the two enzyme totals, and substrate total), the steady-state modified fraction u = [S*]/S_T obeys, at symmetric saturation κ = K_m/S_T,

$$\xi = \frac{u}{1-u}\cdot\frac{\kappa + 1 - u}{\kappa + u},$$

where ξ is the dimensionless kinase/phosphatase activity ratio. Two limits frame the behavior: κ → ∞ gives the graded Michaelian response u = ξ/(1+ξ), and κ → 0 gives the zero-order ultrasensitive switch, in which u jumps from 0 to 1 at ξ = 1 with log–log slope (Hill coefficient) diverging as n_H → ∞.

**Theorem 1 (degeneracy group).** *The steady-state input–output curve depends on the six physical parameters only through two dimensionless groups (ξ at fixed titration coordinate, κ). The degeneracy group is therefore four-dimensional; in particular, all absolute molecular scales (S_T, E_1T, E_2T, and absolute K_m) are structurally unidentifiable from steady-state curve fitting.* Numerically, curve invariance under the group action is exact (max |Δu| = 0 over the full curve).

**Theorem 2 (Fisher barrier).** *At realistic measurement noise, the Fisher information over curve data stratifies the parameters into an identifiable shape tier and a degenerate scale tier: the dimensionless shape parameters (ln c, ln κ) have condition number χ ≈ 30, while the absolute-parameter block has an exact zero eigenvalue (effective condition number ~10^300). No increase in sample size or precision crosses this barrier; only an orthogonal observable does.*

**Theorem 3 (one-sided protocol for deep saturation).** *The local Hill slope of the curve obeys the exact closed form*

$$n_H = 1 + \frac{1}{2\kappa} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \kappa = \frac{1}{2(n_H - 1)},$$

*so κ is recoverable from curve shape alone. However, for κ ≲ 0.01 (n_H ≳ 50) the 95% confidence width on ln κ diverges (≥ 3000-fold at κ = 10^-3), while a plateau of near-identical fits extends to κ → 0. The meaningful statement in this regime is the scan-based one-sided bound κ ≤ κ_max (equivalently n_H ≥ n_H,min), directly analogous to the bound κ ≥ 1 we established for flat-likelihood directions in assay data [TCS paper].*

**Orthogonal resolution.** The scale degeneracy is broken by titrating the orthogonal axis: substrate titration and enzyme titration impose independent constraints, and their intersection identifies the absolute groups K_m and S_T. Thus the absolute scale is not unknowable—it is invisible to any single-axis experiment.

**Information structure.** The audit also settles how the cycle should sit on its own κ-axis if it serves as a sensor. Computing the steady-state mutual information I(ξ; u) between a log-normal input ensemble and the noisy output (observation noise 3%, Western-blot scale; discretization-converged to ±0.02 bits), we find an interior optimum: κ* ≈ 0.05–0.1 for a narrow input distribution (2.85 bits) shifting to κ* ≈ 1–3 for a wide one (3.04 bits), while the zero-order limit is information-poor for all input widths (1.1–1.4 bits). The infinite-gain switch transmits little more than one bit per snapshot: it answers "above or below threshold" but sacrifices the dynamic range needed to say by how much. We read this as a validation of the audit framework against the established principle that optimal response curves match their input statistics [Tkačik & ten Wolde, 2025; Walczak, 2010; Govern & ten Wolde, 2014], and as a quantitative complement to trajectory-level analyses showing that ultrasensitive pathways can transmit maximal information over time [Micali & Endres, 2019]: per steady-state readout, the switch is a decision element, not a measurement element.

## 4. The dynamic encoder: pulse observables select circuit topology

The p53 tumor suppressor responds to DNA double-strand breaks with discrete pulses whose amplitude and duration are approximately dose-independent while their number increases with dose [Lahav, 2004; Batchelor, 2008; Mönke, 2017]—a digital encoding scheme. We asked what this encoding signature implies about the underlying circuit architecture. The answer is a topology-selection theorem: the pulse observables themselves discriminate the circuit's universality class, and they exclude pure negative-feedback models as sufficient explanations of the p53 data.

**Setup: two universality classes and three signatures.** We partition pulse-generator circuits into two universality classes by their interaction graph: the **NF class** (no positive feedback loop; includes the Bar–Or, Monk, and Geva–Zatorsky model families I–III) and the **EXC class** (excitable; contains a positive loop or equivalent fast autocatalysis). From a pulse train under dose drive D we extract three signatures: (i) **onset continuity** of the amplitude–dose relation A(D) at threshold D_c; (ii) **hysteresis width**, the difference between onset and extinction points under up- versus down-scans of D; (iii) the **range sensitivity** α = ∂ ln A/∂ ln D away from onset, with the counting index ν = ∂N/∂ ln D as the complementary digital observable.

**Lemma 1 (NF onset is continuous and analog).** By the Thomas–Soulé theorem, a circuit without a positive loop has at most one steady state. Generic codimension-1 destabilization of a unique steady state is a Hopf bifurcation (a fold would create additional steady states, contradicting uniqueness; transcritical/pitchfork onsets are non-generic). The supercritical Hopf normal form then forces A(D) ∝ √(D−D_c), so that dA/dD diverges at onset: every NF circuit possesses a dose range of graded, strongly dose-dependent amplitude. The sole escape hatch—subcritical Hopf onset—produces finite amplitude at onset but inevitably with nonzero hysteresis (coexisting unstable and stable cycles) and α > 0 above onset; it is caught by signatures (ii) and (iii). Numerically, the canonical delayed three-variable NF model shows a unique destabilization point (s_H = 16.4449, complex pair ±0.924i crossing) with the limit-cycle branch obeying A² ∝ (s−s_H) to R² = 0.9926 (continuation-grade numerics, Methods); the subcritical normal form reproduces finite onset with hysteresis width 0.275 (theory 0.25).

**Lemma 2 (excitable onset is all-or-none).** For time-scale-separated systems ẋ = f(x,w), ẇ = εg(x,w,D) in which the fast subsystem ẋ = f(x,w) (w frozen) loses its attracting branch at folds w₋, w₊, the excursion amplitude is set by the fast-subsystem branch geometry at the folds. When the drive enters only the slow equation, A = x(w₋) − x(w₊) is **independent of D exactly** in the singular limit, with O(ε) corrections (numerically: 6.5% amplitude variation over a 4.6-fold drive range, α = 0.014). When the drive also enters the fast equation, the fast nullcline moves with D and the amplitude acquires a weak drive dependence—the *leaky-digital* regime (Morris–Lecar: α = −0.258, still an order of magnitude below the analog signature). By the Thomas theorem used in Lemma 1, the coexistence of a rest state and a finite excursion requires the positive loop; all-or-none pulsing is therefore architectural, not parametric.

**Lemma 3 (the counting law).** Under exponentially repairing damage D(t) = D₀exp(−t/τ_r) with τ_r ≫ T (period), pulses fire at period T while the drive exceeds threshold I_c, giving N(D₀) ≈ (τ_r/T)·ln(D₀/D_c): dose enters the pulse *count* with logarithmic dynamic range. The law is self-consistent—its fitted slope returns the independently measured period (2.14 vs 2.30 a.u.)—so the counting slope is itself an identifiability tool.

**Theorem (three-signature discrimination).** *The triple (onset continuity; hysteresis width; range α) uniquely assigns a pulse generator to its universality class: NF-supercritical (continuous √-onset; no hysteresis; α ≳ 0.5, diverging at onset), NF-subcritical (finite onset; hysteresis > 0; α > 0), EXC (finite onset; no hysteresis; |α| ≲ 0.3). No single signature suffices; the joint triple is robust to measurement noise at experimental (≲15%) amplitude precision.*

**Experimental placement on the discrimination diagram.** Published single-cell statistics place the p53 DNA-damage response deep in the excitable region. Across the 100-fold dose range of γ-irradiation in ref. [Lahav 2004] (0.1–10 Gy), pulse amplitude was reported dose-independent (|α| ≲ 0.03 for ≤15% amplitude variation), while pulse number increased from ~1–2 to ~5–6 (ν ≈ 2 pulses per decade); the NCS dose series (25–400 ng ml⁻¹) of ref. [Mönke 2017] gives the same placement. Critically, the signature–topology correspondence is subject to a built-in experimental control within a single study: in ref. [Batchelor 2011], the same protein in the same cell line shows excitable, fixed-amplitude, dose-counting pulses in response to double-strand breaks, but a non-excitable, graded single pulse whose amplitude grows in proportion to dose (α ≈ 1, ν ≈ 0) in response to UV. The co-variation of the excitability classification (established experimentally in that work) with the pulse signature (predicted here) holds exactly. We note that population-averaged measurements can mimic analog encoding (damped oscillations with dose-dependent amplitude) through loss of inter-cellular synchrony; the discrimination must therefore be performed on single-cell trajectories.

**Corollary.** The core p53 pulse generator must contain a positive-feedback (or equivalent autocatalytic) module; pure negative-feedback models are excluded as *sufficient* architectures by the pulse data themselves, converting a two-decade modeling intuition into a signature-based exclusion. This is consistent with the documented positive loops in the p53 network (p53 auto-activation; interlinked ATM–p53–Wip1 loops) and with the structure of models that reproduce uniform pulses [Ciliberto 2005; Chickarmane; Mönke 2017]. The same three signatures are measurable in any pulsing signaling system (NF-κB, p53 isoforms, ERK), making the theorem a general circuit-diagnostic tool.

## 5. The digital limit is one limit

**Two paths, one limit.** The static audit (§3) and the dynamic audit (§4) each isolate a regime in which measurement becomes calibration-free, reached by opposite means: in the assay and the covalent cycle, by chemical saturation (κ → 0); in the pulse encoder, by circuit topology (α → 0). We now show these are the same limit in the only sense that matters for inference. In both regimes three things happen together:

1. **The analog scale factor cancels from the likelihood.** In digital counting assays, the effective molecular scale (the calibration factor linking signal to molecule number) drops out of the partition statistics [TCS paper]. In the pulse encoder, the amplitude scale—protein production rates, fluorophore maturation, imaging gain, and every cell-to-cell proportionality—drops out of the pulse-count statistics: N(D₀) ≈ (τ_r/T)·ln(D₀/D_c) contains no amplitude parameter at all.
2. **The observable statistics reduce to counting.** Partition counts in the assay (binomial/Poisson); pulse counts with jittered timing in the encoder. The residual stochasticity in both cases is combinatorial, not parametric.
3. **Only dimensionless ratios survive.** λ-ratios in the assay; D₀/D_c and τ_r/T in the encoder. Absolute molecular scales remain in the degenerate group in both cases—and in both cases they are recoverable only through an orthogonal measurement (§3).

**Why counting is the unique calibration-free channel.** The audit's three-tier stratification explains why this limit exists and why it is unique. Analog observables (amplitudes, intensities, affinities in absolute units) live in the degenerate directions of parameter space: they are corrupted by exactly those factors that no within-system measurement can recalibrate. Counts are invariant under the entire scale subgroup of the degeneracy group—integer events carry no scale to calibrate. Hence any information that must survive parameter uncertainty—uncertainty about the instrument in the assay case, about the cell in the encoder case—has exactly one channel available: counting. Calibration-free information transfer and digital encoding are not two facts but one.

**Decision elements and measurement elements.** This perspective reorganizes two celebrated results. The zero-order ultrasensitive switch [Goldbeter & Koshland, 1981] sits at the digital limit of the *static* channel and is therefore information-poor as a sensor (≈1 bit per snapshot): it is a decision element, reporting threshold crossing with maximal reliability precisely because its output is scale-free. The p53 pulse train sits at the digital limit of the *dynamic* channel and encodes dose in counts with logarithmic range: a measurement element, robust because it never exposes an analog scale. Between the two limits lies the analog regime—information-rich (the static channel's interior optimum κ* of §3) but calibration-hungry, usable only where the degenerate scales are pinned by orthogonal measurements or tight regulation.

**Universality: engineering and evolution converge.** Assay engineers reached counting through metrology (digital PCR as the unique calibration-free platform [TCS paper]); evolution reached it through circuit topology (excitable pulse encoders). The same limit appears on a third level, in computation theory: rate-independent chemical computation—computation robust to kinetic-parameter uncertainty—is exactly the class of piecewise rational linear functions [Chen–Doty–Soloveichik, 2023], and becomes Turing-universal with inhibition. Measurement, encoding, and computation each possess a digital limit in which parameter ignorance is neutralized; the audit identifies the shared structure as the cancellation of analog scale factors. We conjecture that any biological information channel operating under unresolved parameter uncertainty is confined to this limit—a principle that predicts digital architectures wherever calibration is impossible, and explains their prevalence where it is not.

**Scope.** Our claims concern steady-state and single-readout channels. Trajectory-level information can exceed snapshot capacity—switches included [Micali & Endres, 2019]—and the pulse encoder itself illustrates the point: its counting channel is read over time. The digital-limit principle bounds what is *robustly* transferable per unit of calibration effort, not what is transferable in principle.

## 6. Discussion

**What the audit adds.** The information-theoretic literature on biochemical circuits has established how much information flows and what architectures optimize the flow [Tkačik & ten Wolde, 2025]. The audit addresses the complementary epistemic question: what the flow can reveal about the circuit. Its output—a three-tier stratification with reporting protocols—does not compete with information optimization; it disciplines any attempt to infer the optimizer's parameters from data. Likewise, the degeneracy literature in neuronal and cellular models has documented that behavior underdetermines parameters [Prinz, 2004; Goaillard & Marder, 2021]; the audit turns this observation into a computable object (the degeneracy group and its dimension) and a reporting discipline (identifiable combinations, one-sided bounds, orthogonal measurements), so that "parameters are poorly constrained" is replaced by exactly which combinations are constrained, and how well.

**Consistency across systems.** The framework's signature–topology correspondence is consistent with established physiology beyond the p53 case. (i) In Xenopus oocyte maturation—the textbook irreversible cell-fate decision—the MAPK cascade responds with Hill coefficient ≳ 35 and positive feedback, i.e., a near-digital static switch (κ ≲ 0.015 by the n_H = 1 + 1/(2κ) relation) used as a decision element; in mammalian contexts the same ERK pathway responds in a graded manner, confirming that the analog/digital classification is a property of the operating regime, not of molecular identity. (ii) The NF-κB core circuit is a delayed negative-feedback loop without a positive loop; by Lemma 1 it cannot occupy the digital-pure regime, and indeed its dose encoding is analog—carried by amplitude and duration rather than by frequency—with snapshot amplitude information below one bit. (iii) At the tissue level, radioresistant tissues (intestine) show oscillatory p53 (digital, measurement mode: count the damage, repair proportionally), whereas radiosensitive tissues (thymus, spleen) show sustained p53 (switch, decision mode: commit to apoptosis)—the encoding architecture matches physiological function. These consistencies are post hoc, but they are also falsifiable in advance: the framework predicts that any newly characterized pulsing system will show signature–topology co-variation.

**The gauge principle.** The audit's structural consequence, introduced in §2, deserves emphasis. Because observables depend only on dimensionless combinations, the functionally complete description of a circuit lives in dimensionless coordinates; absolute molecular scales are a redundant gauge. Three practices follow. Experimentally, perturbations and cross-system comparisons are meaningful only when stated on dimensionless groups—comparing absolute rate constants across cell types conflates gauge with physics. Evolutionarily, mutation walks in absolute coordinates while selection reads dimensionless ones; the degeneracy group is thus the neutral drift space available to a circuit, which formalizes the observation that ion-channel degeneracy supports resilience [Goaillard & Marder, 2021]. And functionally, the principle rationalizes why biological sensing is so often ratio-based—fold-change detection (the biological Weber law), morphogen-gradient scaling by tissue size, and the kinase/phosphatase activity ratio of §3: a cell has no external calibration standard, so evolution can only have built its sensors on dimensionless quantities. Counting, the digital limit of §5, is the limiting case in which the observable is dimensionless by construction.

**Limitations.** Four bound the present claims. First, our information statements concern steady-state and single-readout channels; trajectory-level information can exceed snapshot capacity [Micali & Endres, 2019], and the pulse encoder itself is read over time—the digital-limit principle bounds robustly transferable information per unit of calibration effort, not information in principle. Second, two proofs are representative rather than fully general: Lemma 2 is stated for time-scale-separated systems with fast-subsystem folds, and Lemma 1's Hopf-onset argument is genericity-based, with the subcritical exception handled by the additional signatures rather than excluded a priori. Third, we audit two circuits; the framework's generality is asserted by construction but demonstrated only in these cases (NF-κB and ERK are the natural next objects). Fourth, the experimental placement on the discrimination diagram is semi-quantitative, derived from published verbal statistics; exact digitization of the source figures will tighten the α bounds (Methods).

**Outlook.** The onset-bifurcation classification of encoding has a thirty-year-old precedent in a neighboring field: neuronal excitability is classified by the same bifurcations (Type I/SNIC versus Type II/Hopf [Rinzel & Ermentrout, 1989]), with the analog signature living in the frequency rather than the amplitude observable. This suggests a general duality—encoding signature as a function of (bifurcation type × observable)—unifying molecular and electrical pulse codes, and points to the degeneracy-group structure of conductance-based neuronal models as the next audit object. More broadly, if the digital-limit conjecture holds, it is a design principle: synthetic circuits that must transmit dose under unresolvable parameter uncertainty should be built to count. The same principle may rationalize, and perhaps guide, robustness choices wherever analog components suffer mismatch—including engineered ones.

## Methods

**Steady states and degeneracy verification (PdPC).** The Goldbeter–Koshland steady state was solved from ξ = u/(1−u)·(κ+1−u)/(κ+u) by brentq (xtol = 10⁻¹⁴); the κ → 0 limit was evaluated analytically (unit step at ξ = 1). Degeneracy-group actions were applied parametrically (rate rescaling; (K_m, S_T) co-scaling; mixed), and curve deviations were evaluated on a 200-point logarithmic titration grid; invariance held to machine precision (max |Δu| = 0).

**Fisher analysis.** The Fisher information matrix was computed from analytic response Jacobians of u(ξ; ln c, ln κ) and of the absolute-parameter block at observation noise σ = 0.03 (Western-blot scale). Confidence widths for ln κ at deep saturation were obtained by Monte Carlo refitting (n = 400 synthetic curves per κ; 95% CI from percentile).

**Mutual information.** The steady-state channel was discretized: input ensemble log-normal in ξ (σ_ξ = 0.3 narrow, 1.5 wide), Gaussian output noise σ = 0.03, adaptive grids over (x, u). Reported values converged across 300/600/1200-point grids to ±0.02 bits.

**Bifurcation analysis (NF family).** For the three-variable model ṗ = s − δ_p p − γmp/(K+p), ẋ = ap − δ_x x, ṁ = bx − δ_m m (δ_p = 0.02, γ = 1.5, K = 0.15, a = b = 1, δ_x = δ_m = 0.35), the equilibrium branch is closed-form, s(p*) = δ_p p* + γm*p*/(K+p*) with x* = ap*/δ_x, m* = abp*/(δ_xδ_m). Stability was scanned along the branch (6000 points; 3×3 analytic Jacobian); the unique destabilization (s_H = 16.4449) occurs via complex pair ±0.924i. Limit-cycle amplitudes were measured by direct integration (solve_ivp, rtol = 10⁻⁹, atol = 10⁻¹²; 60% transient discarded); A² ∝ (s−s_H) holds with R² = 0.9926.

**Subcritical normal form.** ż = (μ+i)z + |z|²z − |z|⁴z (Re c₁ = +1; quintic stabilization), integrated at rtol = 10⁻¹⁰. Onset/extinction: up-scan from r₀ = 0.05, down-scan from r₀ = 1.2; hysteresis width 0.275 (theory 0.25).

**Morris–Lecar.** Standard Type-II parameters (C = 20; g_Ca = 4.4, g_K = 8, g_L = 2; V_Ca = 120, V_K = −84, V_L = −60; v₁ = −1.2, v₂ = 18, v₃ = 2, v₄ = 30; φ = 0.04). Drive I in the voltage equation; spike amplitude = mean peak − window minimum over the stationary window (40% transient discarded).

**Stochastic pulse population.** Excitable reduction (FitzHugh–Nagumo form, a = 0.7, b = 0.8, ε = 0.08, speed factor 20) with damage input I(D,t) = (0.40+0.09D)·t/(t+0.5)·exp(−t/20), integrated by Euler–Maruyama (dt = 0.001; dynamical noise σ = 0.10; extrinsic cell-to-cell variation: lognormal gain, σ = 0.22, and onset-time jitter, SD = 0.18). 200 cells per dose; pulses: prominence ≥ 1.0, minimum spacing 1.0 a.u. The time unit was calibrated by median inter-pulse interval = 5.5 h.

**Counting law.** Fit N vs ln D₀ over the sustained-counting window (N ≥ 2); self-consistency checked by comparing the period inferred from the slope (τ_r/slope) against the directly measured inter-pulse interval.

**Experimental placement.** Semi-quantitative (α, ν) bounds were derived from published statistics: dose ranges 0.1–10 Gy [Lahav 2004] and 25–400 ng ml⁻¹ NCS [Mönke 2017] with amplitude reported dose-independent (|α| bounded by the amplitude-variation ceiling over the log-dose range; ν from reported count ranges); UV proportionality [Batchelor 2011] gives α ≈ 1, ν ≈ 0. Exact digitization of the source figures (WebPlotDigitizer) will tighten these bounds and is deposited in SI when complete.

**Code availability.** All analysis scripts (PdPC audit; bifurcation suite; stochastic population simulation; discrimination diagram) are deposited as supplementary software, each self-contained and deterministic (fixed seeds where stochastic).
